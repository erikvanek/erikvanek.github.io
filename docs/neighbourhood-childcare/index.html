Case study · Ministry of Labour and Social Affairs · 2023–2024

Four children. One neighbour. A law that didn't exist yet.

From April 2023 I worked as the service designer on Sousedské dětské skupiny, neighbourhood children's groups: a new Czech childcare service where a caregiver looks after up to four children in their own home. We had to design and pilot it before the law that allowed it had passed.

01 · The gap
130,000

missing places.

That was the labour minister's estimate of the shortfall in kindergartens and children's groups across Czechia. In Vysočina, where we piloted, many small villages have no kindergarten at all, so parents have to commute for childcare.

02 · The idea

Care in a neighbour's home, capped at four.

A caregiver looks after at most four children, their own included, at home or in another suitable space, and receives a contribution from the state. The model borrows from the German and Austrian Tagesmutter. Parents get flexible care close by. The caregiver gets an income.

In the field, each summit holds at most four presences. The cap is in the code, the same way it is in the law.

03 · The real question

Not whether one home can do it. Whether a region can grow them.

One home is easy to imagine. The hard part is everything around it: how someone learns they qualify, how their home gets checked, who supports them on a bad week, and what a parent needs to know before leaving a two-year-old with a neighbour. That system was the design problem.

04 · Designing ahead of the law

Research first, because the rules were still being written.

After the hydroponic farming startup I'd worked at ended, I deliberately picked a domain I knew nothing about. I joined the ministry's social-innovation team to bring human-centred design into the project. The law was still in drafting, so what we learned from caregivers and parents could still shape it.

05 · The pilot

A sandbox with parents in the room.

The pilot ran for about three months in Vysočina. Because the law hadn't passed, parents had to stay with their children the whole time. We were testing a service whose core promise, leaving your child with a neighbour, couldn't legally happen yet. The sandbox still gave policy what it needed: real households, real home checks, real paperwork.

06 · From pilot to law

Three years, one amendment.

The law was originally meant to take effect on 1 January 2024. It arrived on 1 May 2025, after the pilot had finished.

On 10 April 2025 the jury picked 11 winners in six categories from more than 120 nominations. Our team won in Inovace ve službách veřejnosti, innovation in public services.

07 · Recognition

An award I didn't expect.

When the nomination email arrived in spring 2025, I didn't think we stood much of a chance. I saw the project's outcome as a failure. We won anyway, and with some distance I'm grateful for it.

Thanks above all to Roman and Radka, whose drive moved the project at all, and to the whole team for holding each other up when there was more than enough on everyone's plate.

08 · The honest part

29 of an expected 5,000.

The final evaluation named the likely reasons before launch.

  1. MoneyCaregivers doubted the contribution would cover real costs, and the financing was not yet sustainable.
  2. Start-up costsAdapting a home was a major barrier, and the pilot produced no estimate of what it costs.
  3. No substituteThere was no replacement care for when a caregiver is absent.

We ran regular adoption-risk assessments from the research data throughout the project. Naming a risk is not the same as being able to remove it. Inside a ministry, opinions often carry more weight than data, and a pilot can't change how much a service pays.

The method held up in the evaluation. The economics around it were never something a pilot could test.

A pilot can prove a service works. It can't make people choose it.
