My tried and tested recipe for basic kombucha. I arrived at this method over more than a year of continuously brewing all kinds of kombucha. The recipe makes about 2 litres.

## Ingredients

- 100 g granulated or caster sugar
  - brown sugar works too, but white suits kombucha better
- a good [SCOBY](#scoby), ideally weighing at least 20 g
- 25 g of tea of your choice (black, jasmine, maté, …)
- about 250 ml of [backslop](#backslop) (kombucha from a previous batch, the liquid your SCOBY came in, or some kombucha you buy in a shop)
- a 2.5 l glass jar with a neck you can cover with cloth and seal with a rubber band
- 2 l of water

## 10 steps to great kombucha

1. make the tea according to its instructions, stir in the sugar and let it cool to room temperature
2. pour it into the glass jar, add the backslop and stir
3. add the SCOBY, cover the neck with cloth and seal it with a rubber band
   - at first the SCOBY may sink to the bottom, turn around and move about in the liquid - that’s completely fine
   - after a while it will probably float up to the surface
   - over time a new SCOBY may well start forming on the surface - you can use it in further batches or share it with someone 🙌
4. keep the jar at normal room temperature; it shouldn’t drop below 15 or rise much above 30 degrees, and somewhere around 20 is ideal
5. let it ferment for about 2 weeks and taste it along the way
   - how sour it ends up is up to you; some like it less, some more - the longer the kombucha ferments, the sourer it gets
6. when the kombucha tastes the way you want, take out the SCOBY and strain the liquid through a sieve to remove sediment and any bits of SCOBY
7. pour some aside as [backslop](#backslop) for your next kombucha
8. then bottle it
   - I’d recommend bottles with a resealable cap; old Vincentka mineral water bottles have worked well for me
   - don’t fill the bottle to the very top; leave 2-3 cm of air so the bottle doesn’t explode
   - if you want the drink to sparkle, try a [secondary fermentation](#secondary)
9. cold stops the fermentation, so keep the finished drink in the fridge or somewhere cool; otherwise it keeps fermenting and gets sourer and sourer
10. and repeat the whole process again and again and again, and share the results 🍿

<h3 id="scoby">🍄 SCOBY</h3>

- SCOBY stands for symbiotic culture of bacteria and yeast
- you can buy them in health food shops, for example, but unfortunately they’re often not in good shape
- I recommend getting in touch with someone you know brews kombucha and trying to get a SCOBY from a practising fermenter
- a SCOBY grows and multiplies over time, so it’s easy to share
- as the SCOBY grows, it can “ferment” more and more liquid
  - a 20 g kombucha, for example, probably won’t manage to ferment 5 l of liquid, but it might manage 2 l
  - in my experience a strong SCOBY weighs at least 100 g; at that point it easily handles a 4 l batch and produces new offshoots
- a healthy, active SCOBY is supple, glossy, and white to yellowish brown

<h3 id="backslop">Backslop</h3>

- this is kombucha that’s already made, which contains the microorganisms you need and has the right pH
- it helps create ideal conditions for fermentation in the new batch
- you’ll probably get it in one of these ways:
  - pour it off from the previous fermentation cycle
  - buy some unpasteurised and ideally unsweetened kombucha in a shop, for example in a health food shop
  - a SCOBY will quite likely be floating in some kombucha when you first bring it home
    - that amount may not be enough for a new batch, so I’d recommend combining it with some shop-bought kombucha
- it works much like adding a sourdough starter to dough when baking

<h3 id="secondary">Secondary fermentation</h3>

- a step that lets us both flavour the finished drink and carbonate it with CO<sub>2</sub>, giving it a pleasant sparkle
- when bottling, we add a source of sugar to the bottles, which starts the fermentation again → hence secondary fermentation
- we can add, for example:
  - sugar
  - honey
  - molasses
  - chopped fruit (pear, mango, cherries, …)
  - dried fruit (raisins, …)
  - a homemade syrup
- as for ratios, this is more about your own experiments
- I add about 6% of the final volume; for example, when I bottle into a one-litre bottle, I add 60 g of chopped pear
- how long the secondary fermentation takes depends a lot on the sugar source and how easily it releases into the liquid
  - plain sugar or honey, for example, ferments very quickly, berries such as gooseberries more slowly, because the sugar releases gradually
  - in the pear example above, I’d let the secondary fermentation run for about 2-3 days, again at room temperature
  - a good test of how far the secondary fermentation has got: turn the bottle upside down and back, and you should see nice chains of bubbles rising inside
- **CAREFUL** - the biggest risk when experimenting with secondary fermentation is a bottle exploding - it has never happened to me with kombucha, but the risk is there, so be careful
